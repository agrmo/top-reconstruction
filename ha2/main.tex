\section*{Problem 1}
The best way to approach this question is to list the fundamental particles in the Standard Model, and explain which ones are not relevant to interactions in a typical chemistry laboratory. The Standard Model describes the following bosons, some of which are more relevant to chemistry than others.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Photons. Photons are a necessary aspect of chemistry experiments and can be excited at low energies. The QED sector of the Standard Model and its calssical limits provide a good foundation for these interactions.
\item Gluons. Gluons are obviously necessary for subatomic bonding of quarks. However single gluon emission into mesons is not kinematically allowed in almost all chemistry laboratories, as the lowest mass meson in the Pion. So Gluonic interactions are only relevant in the chemistry laboratory for baryonic binding purposes, but not for $g\rightarrow FF$ processes.
\item W bosons. W bosons are relevant to chemistry in $\beta$ decays of radioactive elements. More broadly speaking, chemical interactions using W bosons are relevant only when there is a clear decay process that reduces the potential energy of the system, including for example $\beta$ decays.
\fig{4cm}{14.png}{From the Standard Model, W bosons are crucial in explaining $\beta$ decays in chemistry.}
However W bosons are not relevant in chemistry for exciting W bosons which decay into baryon or meson states. This is because almost all chemical reactions do not provide nearly enouch energy to excite a W boson with enough energy to decay into hadronic states.
\fig{4cm}{15.png}{From the Standard Model, mesonic decays of W bosons are not relevant in almost all chemistry environments, such as in the process $n\rightarrow p \pi^{-}$.}
\item Z bosons. Z bosons are not relevant to chemistry experiments, becaues their excitation requires high energy, around 90 GeV.
\fig{5cm}{16.png}{Production of leptons and mesons from the Z boson is only significant when the center of mass energy is significant enough for those final state particles to be kinematically allowed. The process in this diagram is relevant at high energy colliders such as LEP, but not relevant, or even kinematically allowed, in a chemistry laboratory.}
\end{enumerate}
The Standard Model also includes the following fermions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Up quarks and down quarks. These particles are crucial in the chemistry laboratory for construction of protons and neutrons. The SM also provides a well established model for $\beta$ interactions as already explained above.
\item Charm quarks, strange quarks, top quarks, bottom quarks and their composite states are not relevant in the chemistry, because their excitation energies are well above what a typical chemistry laboratory must create. Case in point, the discovery of the lightest of these particles, the charm quark, was only made readily abundant at SLAC and BNL in 1974. SLAC used an $e^+ e^-$ collider at $s=3.1$ GeV, and BNL used a proton collider at 30 GeV.
\end{enumerate}

\item Muons and taus are not excitable in a laboratory scenario. Even for high energy $\beta$ decays where the W boson decays electrons, the final state electrons are almost never relevant in nuclear chemistry due to their scarcity per unit time.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Problem 6.c.}
The process $\Delta^{+}\rightarrow n \pi^+$ is an \fbox{electromagnetic process}. The process $\Sigma^0 \rightarrow \Lambda^0 \gamma$ is an \fbox{electromagnetic process}. The process $\pi^{+} \rightarrow \mu^{+} \nu_\mu$ is a \fbox{weak process}. The process $\pi^0\rightarrow \gamma \gamma$ is an \fbox{electromagnetic process}. Electromagnetic processes typically have a shorter lifetime than weak processes, so based on the interaction type alone, the process order in increasing lifetime is
\begin{enumerate}
\item \fbox{$\Delta^{+}\rightarrow n \pi^+$ and $\Sigma^0 \rightarrow \Lambda^0 \gamma$ and $\pi^0\rightarrow \gamma \gamma$}
\item \fbox{$\pi^{+} \rightarrow \mu^{+} \nu_\mu$}
\end{enumerate}
From the PDG, the process lifetimes are
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\Delta^{+}\rightarrow n \pi^+$ \fbox{$\tau = 5.57\tun{-24}{s}$}
\item $\Sigma^0 \rightarrow \Lambda^0 \gamma$ \fbox{$\tau = 7.4\tun{-20}{s}$}
\item $\pi^{+} \rightarrow \mu^{+} \nu_\mu$ \fbox{$\tau = 2.603\tun{-8}{s}$}
\item $\pi^0\rightarrow \gamma \gamma$ \fbox{$\tau = 8.52\tun{-17}{s}$}
\end{enumerate}
Which supports the ordering. We can refine the ordering by looking at the mass difference of the three electromagnetic processes. The mass differences for electromagnetic processes are 152.87 MeV, 33.92 MeV, and 134.976 MeV. So we can order these three processes in order of decreasing mass difference. This gives the ordering
\begin{enumerate}
\item \fbox{$\Delta^{+}\rightarrow n \pi^+$}
\item \fbox{$\Sigma^0 \rightarrow \Lambda^0 \gamma$}
\item \fbox{$\pi^0\rightarrow \gamma \gamma$}
\item \fbox{$\pi^{+} \rightarrow \mu^{+} \nu_\mu$}
\end{enumerate}
Which agrees with the experimental $\tau$ lifetimes.
