\section*{Problem 1}
Being an airline pilot, you are already familiar with shortest paths, or geodesics, on Earth. For the purpose of flight navigation it's crucial to know the distance between two latitude, longitude pairs $(\phi_1,\lambda_1)$ and $(\phi_2,\lambda_2)$. For very short distances, it's appropriate to approximate the coordinates as lying on a flat plane. For larger distances, it may be appropriate to approximate the Earth as a perfect sphere. And finally, to be more accurate, it may be appropriate to approximate the Earth as an ellipsoid. Because the Earth bulges outwards at the equator, moreso than a sphere, the ellipsoid approximation is helpful for paths that traverse large amounts of longitude.\\~\\
In astronomy planets typically follow orbits in a flat, two dimensional plane. Over billions of years, planets fall into planar orbits due to the conservation of their angular momentum. Most other chaotic, perhaps oscillatory, non-conserved motion is typically lost. When this plane is intersected with a sphere, they draw circles, also called great circles. However this sphere is purely hypothetical: in astronomy this hypothetical sphere is called the celestial sphere, and the coordinates thereon are simply used to describe the motion of other stars and planets in an idealized way. Thus, these great circles are merely projections on an idealized sphere. In reality, the paths of planets are typically elliptic, especially true in our solar system.\\~\\
Finally you mentioned you were interested in relativity. When physicists speak of geodesics in the context of relativity, they are not speaking just about paths in three dimensions of space, as you have become accustomed to in aviation. Instead, these paths are shortest in spacetime. The lengths of these paths are not purely added together, such as $ds^2=dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2$. Instead, there is a minus sign: $ds^2=-dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2$. This leads to many results about shortest paths in spacetime that are inconceivable in a purely spatial sense. For example, the \textit{longest} spacetime path is typically the one where an object is \textit{for the entire duration} at rest. These results take more pondering to solidify intuitively. Hopefully this helps introduce you to some concepts in relativity.
\section*{Problem 2.a.}
Using the unit sphere metric
\g{ds^2=d\theta^2+\sin(\theta)^2d\phi^2}
the coordinates are \fbox{$\theta$ and $\phi$}, angle and azimuthal angle respectively. In cartesian coordinates, the coordinates $x$, $y$ and $z$ are
\g{\boxed{x=\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi) \qquad y=sin(\theta)\sin(\phi) \qquad z=\cos(\theta)}}
And so in cartesian coordinates we have three coordinates and only two in curvilinear coordinates. The components of the metric tensor can be read off from $ds^2$,
\g{\boxed{g^{ij}=\matr{1&0\\ 0&\sin(\theta)^2}}}
The determinant of the metric $g$ is
\g{\boxed{g=\sin(\theta)^2}}
To calculate the coordinate basis vectors $\vec{e}_i$ take derivatives of the cartesian coordinates in the curvilinear directions.
\g{\hat{\theta}&=(\dd{\theta}x)\hat{x}+(\dd{\theta}y)\hat{y}+(\dd{\theta}z)\hat{z}\\
&=\boxed{\cos(\theta)\cos(\phi)\hat{x}+\cos(\theta)\sin(\phi)\hat{y}-\sin(\theta)\hat{z}}}
\g{\hat{\phi}&=(\dd{\phi}x)\hat{x}+(\dd{\phi}y)\hat{y}+(\dd{\phi}z)\hat{z}\\
&=\boxed{-\sin(\theta)\sin(\phi)\hat{x}+\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi)\hat{y}}}
$\hat{\phi}$ has no change in $z$ coordinate, as expected. Around a point $P$ on a manifold, a tangent space can be constructed using the basis vectors
\g{e_\mu = \pa{x^\mu} P}
Then any vector $A$ can be expressed as a value in the tangent space
\g{A=A^\mu \pa{x^\mu} P}
where the direction derivative along $A$ is 
\g{\partial_{A}=A^\mu \pa{x^\mu}}
The components of $A^\mu$ are identical to the coefficients of $A^\mu$ in the coordinate-expansion of the directional derivative $\partial_{A}$, each at the same point $P$. This 1-to-1 correspondence allows one to state that a \fbox{vector can be expressed as a differential operator}.

\section*{Problem 2.d.}
Ref MCP p. 1168. The commutator of two vector fields $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$ is 
\g{[\vec{A},\vec{B}]=[A^\alpha \pa{x^\alpha},B^\beta \pa{x^\beta}]=( A^\alpha \pa{x^\alpha}B^\beta - B^\alpha \pa{x^\alpha} A^\beta )\pa{x^\beta}}
Geometrically, this operation is the construction of the fifth leg which closes the quadrilaeral, whose four legs are the vector fields $\vec{A}$ and $\vec{B}$, or the change in $\vec{B}$ relative to $\vec{A}$. The commutation coefficients are
\g{c_{\alpha \beta}^\rho = \vec{e}^\rho \cdot [\vec{e}_\alpha,\vec{e}_\beta]}
It is clear to see which coefficients will be zero: the ones with commutative derivatives, in other words two of the same lower indices.
\g{\boxed{c_{\theta\theta}^\theta = \br{1}{(\sin(\theta))^3}\pa{\theta}(\ppa{2}{\theta}-\ppa{2}{\theta})=0}}
\g{\boxed{c_{\theta\theta}^\phi= c_{\phi\phi}^\theta=c_{\phi\phi}^\phi=0}}
\g{\boxed{c_{\phi\theta}^\phi = \br{1}{\sin(\phi)}\pa{\phi}(\pa{\phi}\pa{\theta}-\pa{\theta}\pa{\phi})}}
\g{\boxed{c_{\theta\theta}^\phi = \br{1}{\sin(\phi)}\pa{\phi}(\pa{\theta}\pa{\phi}-\pa{\phi}\pa{\theta})}}

\g{\boxed{c_{\phi\theta}^\theta= \br{1}{(\sin(\phi))^2}\pa{\theta}(\pa{\phi}\pa{\theta}-\pa{\theta}\pa{\phi})}}
\g{\boxed{c_{\theta\theta}^\phi = \br{1}{\sin(\phi)}\pa{\theta}(\pa{\theta}\pa{\phi}-\pa{\phi}\pa{\theta})}}
The Lie derivative $[\vec{A},\vec{B}]$ tells us whether the application of two vectors is path dependent. When the commutator is zero, that means there is no difference adding one vector to the head of the other. When the commutator is nonzero, that means the order of application of each vector changes the final resultant vector. The results here tell us that application of vectors in the \fbox{same basis component is path independent}, e.g. $\theta$ to $\theta$, and $\phi$ to $\phi$. Conversely any application of vectors in \fbox{different basis components is path dependent}, e.g. $\phi$ to $\theta$.
