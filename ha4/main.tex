\section*{Problem 3.a.}
We can set up the problem as follows. A detector is placed at point A on the surface of the Earth. The point B is at height $h$ from the surface of the Earth. At point B, $\nu_\mu$ are produced, which travel a distance $x$ to the detector. The $\nu_\mu$ travel a distance $x$ to the detector. If $\theta_z=0$, then the detector is directly underneath the point of production of $\nu_\mu$ so the path $x$ is equal to $h$, the minimum possible value. As the angle $\theta$ increases, the $\nu_\mu$ travel a larger distance $x$. The maximum possible value of $x$ is when $\theta_z=\pi$, where in this case the $\nu_\mu$ are produced on the opposite side of Earth, travel through the center of the Earth, then reach the detector, so $x=2R+h$. Then
\e{x\in [h,2R+h]}
The distance $x$ is must take into account the curvature of the Earth. There following diagram demonstrates several examples.
\fig{5cm}{2.png}{Examples. The $\nu_\mu$ begin at a height $h$, travel at angle $\theta_z$, and travel a distance $x$ to the experiment. As $\theta_z$ increases, the distance $x$ increases.}
So we should expect that $x$ increases significantly as $\theta_z$ increases. Formulate the trigonometric problem with the following diagram.
\fig{7cm}{1.png}{Diagram.}
Now solve for $x$. By simple inspection,
\e{\thcab=\pi-\theta_z}
Now apply the Law of Sines and solve for $\thabc$.
\e{\fr{R+h}{\sin(\thcab)}=\fr{R}{\sin(\thabc)}}
\e{\sin(\thabc)=\fr{R}{R+h}\sin(\thcab)}
\e{\thabc=\arcsin(\fr{R}{R+h}\sin(\pi-\theta_z))}
Now apply the Law of Cosines and solve for $x$.
\e{x&=R\cos(\thcab)+(R+h)\cos(\thabc)\\
&= \boxed{R\cos(\pi-\theta_z)+(R+h)\cos(\arcsin(\fr{R}{R+h}\sin(\pi-\theta_z)))}}
Plug $x$ into $P$. The survival probability for $i$ type particles is simply one minus the probability of mixing into $j$ type particles.
\e{P_i&=1-P_{i\rightarrow j}\\
&=\lab{81n23}\boxed{1-\sin^2(2\theta)\sin^2(\fr{1.267 \Delta m_{ij}^2 \rom{[eV$^2$]} x \rom{[km]}}{E\rom{[GeV]}})}}
Before continuing to the next problem we should consider if this answer is correct. Plot $x$ in a linear plot.
\fig{6cm}{1.pdf}{Linear plot of the distance traveled $x$ as a function of $\theta_z$.}
Plot $x$ in a log plot.
\fig{6cm}{2.pdf}{Log plot of the distance traveled $x$ as a function of $\theta_z$. The two lines are $h$ and $2R+h$, the expected minimum and maximum values of $x$.}
This seems to be a strange result. It is true, that the distance $x$ ranges from $h$ to $2R+h$ as predicted, however the value of $x$ is highly nonlinear as a function of $\theta_z$. In particular, $x$ increases by orders of magnitude at
\e{\theta_z=\fr{\pi}{2}}
What is the reason for this behavior? Upon closer inspection, we see that it is not possible for $\theta_z$ to be greater than $\pi/2$ without ``circling the globe.'' In other words, as soon as $\theta_z$ begins to point into the Earth, the distance will rapidly increase, as a slight change in angle will significantly change the distance required to maintain the required height of $h$. This is easily demonstrated if the reader stands straight, points their arm towards at a fixed height in the sky, then lowers their arm. As the arm lowers, the distance $x$ stays relatively equal to the required height $h$, except, as the arm crosses below the horizon, $x$ must sigificantly increase. The reader's arm now points underneath the Earth, ``circling the globe.'' So we should expect the survival probability of $\nu_\mu$ in Problem 3.b. to change significantly at around $\theta_z=\pi/2$.

We can conclude that the survival probability for a neutrino oscillates less at its energy increases. In other words, neutrinos mix less at higher energy. We can also conclude that as the distance $x$ increases significantly, the survival probability oscillates more significantly. This can be useful for experimental purposes, for example, of only $\nu_\tau$ are desired, then an experimental device can, hypothetically, be built to produce 10 GeV $\nu_\tau$ with a baseline distance of 6000 km. This is essentially the goal of the DUNE experiment, to perform experiments at the first maximum of $P_{\mu e}$.
