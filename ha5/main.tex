\section*{Problem 1}
A symmetry is any transformation of a variable which leaves a function unchanged. There are many examples of symmetries in physics. When a symmetry exists for a function, there exists a vector field whose divergence is zero. These vector fields are called Noether currents. The spatial integral of a Noether currents is called a Noether charge. These conserved quantities are the consequence of the zero divergence of a vector field. The simplest example from physics is the electric field, and the electric charge. The electric field has a zero divergence, and is therefore associated with some conserved quantity, which is called electric charge.

\section*{Problem 3.d.}
Using natural units with $\hbar=c=G$ there is only one combination of $\hbar$, $c$, and $G$ that has units of length. Schematically,
\g{\ein{m}=\hbar^a \ein{[kg m$^2$ s$^{-1}$]} \times c^b \ein{[m s$^{-1}$]} \times G^c \ein{[m$^3$ kg$^{-1}$ s$^{-2}$]}}
$\hbar G$ has units of m$^5$ s$^{-3}$, so $c$ needs to be introduced. $\br{\hbar G}{c^3}$ has units of $m^2$, so the entire expression needs to be square-rooted. The resultant expression for the Planck length is
\g{\boxed{l_P=\sqrt{\br{\hbar G}{c^3}}=1.61\zehnein{-35}{m}}}
Similarly the Planck time can be calculated by observing that $\hbar G$ has units of m$^5$ s$^{-3}$, $\br{\hbar G}{c^5}$ has units of s$^2$, and so the Planck time is
\g{\boxed{\sqrt{\br{\hbar G}{c^5}} = 5.37\zehnein{-44}{s}}}
Similarly the Planck mass can be calculated by observing that $\br{\hbar}{G}$ has units of kg$^2$ m$^{-1}$ s, and then $\br{\hbar c}{G}$ has units of kg$^2$, and so the Planck mass is expressed as
\g{\boxed{\sqrt{\br{\hbar c}{g}}=21.72\ein{$\mu$g}}}
Similarly the Planck energy can be calculated by observing that $\br{\hbar}{G}$ has units of kg$^2$ m$^{-1}$ s, and then $\br{\hbar c^5}{G}$ has units of kg$^2$ m$^4$ s$^{-4}$, and so the Planck energy is
\g{\boxed{\sqrt{\br{\hbar c^5}{G}}=1.9\zehnein{9}{J}=1.18\zehnein{28}{eV}}}
