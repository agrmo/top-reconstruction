\section*{Problem 1}
There are a few types of astrophysical measurements that can be made to demonstrate the exitence of black holes.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Stellar orbits near galactic centers. While galactic centers are typically extremely bright, telescope resolution has improved significantly in recent decades, permitting direct path measurements of stellar orbits near galactic centers. 
\fig{6cm}{2.jpg}{Plot of orbits near the Milky Way galactic center. From the UCLA Galactic Center Group.}
Using only Newtonian gravitation we can estimate the mass of the dark, massive object at the galactic center to be around 200 million solar masses.
\item Weak lensing near galactic centers. Typically, cases of lensing, both weak and strong, are rare and circumstancial: stellar objects must be precisely aligned with Earth in order to detect lensing signals. Nonetheless the catalogue of candidate black holes has increased significantly and, additionally, many cases of microlensing have found. We can demonstrate the existence of black holes by finding a black hole with microlensing effects on its foreground objects. The MACHO collaboration has surveyed the galactic bulge to find a small number of candidate black holes in the Milky Way, away from the galactic center, which have measurable microlensing.
\item Direct radiometric reconstruction. In the past decade two significant attempts have been made to image the black holes within the Milky Way, Sag A*, and in M87, M87*. While M87* is much further away, its black hole is very large, and the converse is true for Sag A*. Imaging of the black holes is currently achieved through networks of radio telescope interferometers.
\end{enumerate}
\section*{Problem 2.a.}
Starting with the constant angular momentum
\g{L=mr^2 \dd{\tau}{\phi}}
We can write the total energy of the object in orbit around a Schwarzschild black hole,
\g{E=\br{1}{2}m(\dd{\tau}r)2-\br{GMm}{r}+\br{L^2}{2mr^2}(1-\br{2GM}{rc^2}}
This is a conserved quantity for any orbit. Using the substitution $s=\br{1}{r}$ the change of variables is used,
\g{\dd{\tau}{r}r=\dd{s}r\dd{\phi}s\dd{\tau}\phi=-\br{1}{s^2}(\dd{\phi}s)\br{L}{mr^2}=-\br{L}{m}\dd{\phi}s}
resulting in the energy expression
\g{E=\br{L^2}{2m}((\dd{\phi}s^2+s^2)-GMms-\br{GML^2}{mc^2}s^3}
Differentiate with respect to $\phi$, divide out the $\dda{\phi}s$ which appears in every term, and then rearrange to give
\g{\boxed{\dda{2}{\phi}s+s=\br{GMm^2}{l^2}+\br{3GM}{c^2}s}}
Starting with energy of the Schwarzschild orbit, and differentiating with respect to $\phi$ gives the desired differential equation.

\section*{Problem 3.a.}
For the Kerr metric, the two dimensional part of the metric is calculated when $r=r_H=1+(1-a^2)^{1/2}$, $\Delta=0$, and $dt=0$. 
\g{ds^2=\rho_H^2 d\theta^2 + \overline{\omega}_H^2d\phi^2}
The $\overline{\omega}^2$ term can be simplified significantly,
\g{\overline{\omega}^2=\br{\Sigma^2 \sin(\theta)^2}{\rho^2}=\br{((1+(1-a^2)^{1/2})^2+a^2)^2\sin(\theta)^2}{\rho^2}=(\br{2r\sin(\theta)}{\rho})^2}
and so the metric is then
\g{ds^2=\rho^2 d\theta^2+(\br{2r}{\rho})^2\sin(\theta)^2 d\phi^2}
This is a \fbox{two dimensional surface element.} We can integrate this two dimensional surface element easily resulting in 
\g{\boxed{A=\int \int ds^2 = 8\pi r_H}}
By starting with the Kerr metric and considering a constant time, constant radius solution at the horizon, we find the metric describes a surface element of an object with surface area $8\pi r_H$.

\section*{Problem 4.a.}
For a Kerr black hole in BL coordinates, there are two event horizons $r_+$ and $r_-$, and one radius further out from $r_+$ named the ergosphere $r_E$. The two event horizons occur when $g_{rr}$ is singular, or when $\Delta=0$.
\g{\Delta=0}
\g{0=r^2-2Mr+a^2}
Quadratic formula.
\g{\boxed{r_\pm=\br{1}{2}(2M\pm(4M^2-4a^2)^{1/2})=M\pm(M^2-a^2)^{1/2}}}
As requested when $a=0.99M$ then $r_\pm$ are
\g{r_+&=\boxed{1.14GM}}
To calculate the radius of the ergosphere, set $g_{tt}$ to 0 and solve for the radius.
\g{1-\br{2Mr}=0}
\g{\rho^2=2Mr}
\g{r^2+a^2\cos^2(\theta)-2Mr=0}
Solve with the quadratic equation,
\g{\boxed{r_E(\theta)=M+(M^2-a^2\cos(\theta)^2)^{1/2}}}
This is the radius of the Ergosphere.

\section*{Problem 4.c.}
The proper time for a stationary observer is
\g{(\Delta \tau)^2=-(\Delta s)^2=-g_{\mu\nu}\Delta x^\mu \Delta x^\nu}
which in the B-L coordinates of the Kerr metric is always negative, due to the lack of displacement in the rest frame, and the fact that $g_{tt}$ is negative outside the Ergosphere, as demonstrated in the previous part. However we learned that within the Ergosphere,
\g{r<r_E}
the $g_{tt}$ term flips sign. This \fbox{poses a contradiction} between $(\Delta \tau)^2<0$ and $g_{tt}>0$. The only way to resolve this is by \fbox{giving the particle nonzero displacement}. Thus with this reasoning there are \fbox{no stationary solutions in the Ergosphere.} In particular looking at the spatial terms in the metric,
\g{ds^2=-(1-\br{2Mr}{\rho^2})dt^2-\br{4M}{\rho^2}ar\sin(\theta)^2 d\phi dt + \br{\rho^2}{\Delta} dr^2 + \rho^2 d\theta^2 + (r^2 + a^2 + \br{2Mra^2\sin(\theta)^2}{\rho^2})\sin(\theta)^2 d\phi^2}
The only negative contribution comes from the $d\phi dt$ term, and so \fbox{$d\phi>0$}, the world line of a test particle must travel in the same direction as the rotating Kerr black hole.
\section*{Problem 4.d.}
The energy of a particle travelling along a geodesic in the Kerr metric is the contraction of the $t$ Killing vector and four momentum,
\g{K=\pa{t}}
\g{p^\mu=m\pa{\tau} x^\mu}
\g{E=-K_\mu p^\mu = m(1-\br{GM}{\rho^2})\pa{\tau}t+\br{2mGMar}{\rho^2}\sin(\theta)^2\pa{\tau}\phi}
Its angular momenutm $L$ is the contraction of the $\phi$ Killing tensor and four momentum,
\g{R=\pa{\phi}}
\g{L=-R_\mu p^\mu = -\br{2mGMar}{\rho^2}\sin(\theta)^2\pa{\tau} t + \br{1}{\rho^2}(m(r^2+a^2)^2-m\Delta a^2\sin(\theta)^2)\sin(\theta)^2\pa{\tau}\phi}
These are calculated at distances far from $r_+$, outside the black hole.
\section*{Problem 4.e.}
The calculation of $E$ demonstrated that the energy of the particle as measured by the large distance observer is positive. Now when the particle traverses into the Ergosphere, $K^\mu=\pa{t}$ changes from timelike to spacelike, thereby flipping the sign of its energy,
\g{\boxed{E=-K^\mu p_\mu < 0}}
This might seem disturbing until we recognize that the energy flips sign again when leaving the Ergosphere. This appears to be an interesting mechanism for transforming a particle to antiparticle.
\section*{Problem 4.f.}
Photons can certainly escape outside of a Kerr Ergosphere via a collision.
\g{\boxed{p_{\gamma i}=p_2^\mu+p_{\gamma f}^\mu}}

The directions of $p_\gamma$ and $p_2$ are oriented such that the photon travels back out of the Ergosphere with enough energy to escape, as described in 4.b.

\g{\boxed{E_{\gamma f}>E_{\gamma i}}}
